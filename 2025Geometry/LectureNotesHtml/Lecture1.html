What is Mathematics at its Core?

Mathematics can be understood through several fundamental perspectives that reveal its true nature and purpose. At its essence, mathematics encompasses the manipulation of numbers and variables, allowing us to quantify and relate abstract concepts. It represents the extension of logic to its absolute limits and sometimes even beyond those boundaries, pushing the frontiers of rational thought.

Mathematics also serves as an abstract representation of the real world, providing us with tools to model and understand natural phenomena. Perhaps most profoundly, it involves the search for and discovery (or invention) of Universal Absolute Truths that transcend cultural and temporal boundaries.

A Small History Lesson: YBC 7289

YBC 7289: A Babylonian clay tablet from approximately 2000 BCE

This remarkable artifact demonstrates the sophistication of ancient mathematical knowledge. The tablet shows a square with its diagonal, and contains cuneiform numbers that represent an extraordinarily accurate approximation of √2.

Babylonian Base-60 Calculation:
1 + 24/60 + 51/3600 + 10/60³ ≈ 1.414212

This calculation yields √2 ≈ 1.414213 to an remarkable number of decimal places, with accuracy within one part in a million. This level of precision far exceeds what would be necessary for any practical engineering or economic application of that era.

The extraordinary precision of this calculation distinguishes this object as pure mathematics rather than applied engineering. It demonstrates that ancient scholars pursued mathematical knowledge for its own sake, seeking accuracy that had no practical application. The image shows the hypotenuse of a square, so if we use the side of the square as a unit, then by the Pythagorean Theorem (formalized around 500 BCE), the diagonal equals √2.

Another Ancient Mathematical Artifact: Plimpton 322

Plimpton 322: Another Babylonian tablet from approximately 2000 BCE

This cuneiform tablet contains extensive integer solutions to the Pythagorean equation x² + y² = z². The tablet demonstrates that ancient Babylonian mathematicians had discovered and systematically catalogued Pythagorean triples, showing their deep understanding of this fundamental mathematical relationship.

The Missing Element: Proof

While these ancient mathematical objects demonstrate remarkable computational sophistication and theoretical understanding, they lack what we now consider essential to mathematics: rigorous proof. There came a transformative time in mathematical history when people no longer accepted mere "facts" as sufficient truth. Instead, they demanded a paired concept: (Truth, Proof).

The earliest known mathematical proof is attributed to Thales around 600 BCE, marking the beginning of mathematics as we understand it today.

Thales's Theorem

Thales's Theorem: Take any circle, draw its diameter, and place any other point on the circle. The angle formed by connecting this point to both endpoints of the diameter will always be a right angle (90°).

Interactive Demonstration of Thales's Theorem

Move point C around the circle to observe that angle ∠BCD remains 90° regardless of C's position

The fundamental question becomes: How does the angle ∠BCD depend on the position of point C? Empirical observation shows that every time we measure this angle, it equals 90°. While Babylonian mathematicians might have been satisfied with stating this observational fact, modern mathematical understanding demands that we prove why this must always be true.

Proving Thales's Theorem

Proof Strategy

Idea: Draw a line from the center A to point C, creating two triangles within the circle.

Key Insight: We can use the fact that the sum of interior angles in any triangle equals 180°.

Analysis: Since AD and AC are both radii of the circle, they are equal in length. Therefore, triangle ADC is isosceles, which means its base angles are equal: ∠ADC = ∠ACD = x.

Similarly, triangle ABC is also isosceles (AB and AC are both radii), so ∠ACB = ∠ABC = y.

The crucial calculation: In triangle BCD, the sum of angles must equal 180°:

∠D + ∠B + (∠DCA + ∠ACB) = 180°

x + y + (x + y) = 180°

2(x + y) = 180°

Therefore: x + y = 90°

Since ∠BCD = ∠DCA + ∠ACB = x + y, we conclude that ∠BCD = 90°.

QED

The Foundation Problem

This proof raises a fundamental philosophical question: If every mathematical fact must be proved using other facts, what validates those supporting facts? This creates a potentially infinite recursion of required proofs, leading to what could be an endless chain of justifications.

Consider the dangerous possibility: suppose that in proving some fact, we unknowingly relied on another "fact" that is actually false, such as 0 = 1. With such a contradiction embedded in our logical foundation, we could "prove" anything, including false statements like the Riemann Hypothesis through the following absurd reasoning:

False "Proof" of the Riemann Hypothesis:

Assume the Riemann Hypothesis is false. Since 0 = 1 (our false assumption), we have a contradiction. Therefore, the Riemann Hypothesis must be true. QED.

Our entire mathematical edifice could collapse if we discover that our chain of logical dependencies ultimately rests on false statements hidden within this infinite recursion of proofs.

The Solution: Axioms

To prevent this logical catastrophe, we must establish a firm foundation by starting with statements that we explicitly declare to be true without proof. These fundamental assumptions are called axioms. Mathematics thus requires not just a pair (Truth, Proof), but rather a triplet: (Axioms, Fact to prove, Proof).

The ancient document that exemplifies this axiomatic ideal is Euclid's Elements, which remains one of the most influential mathematical works ever written.

Euclid's Elements: The Axiomatic Foundation

Euclid's Elements systematically develops geometry from a small set of definitions, postulates, and common notions. Here are some key foundations from this monumental work:

Definitions

Definition 1: A point is that which has no part.

Definition 2: A line is breadthless length. (By "line," Euclid means what we would call a "curve")

Definition 4: A straight line is a line which lies evenly with the points on itself.

Definition 10: When a straight line standing on a straight line makes the adjacent angles equal to one another, each of the equal angles is right, and the straight line standing on the other is called a perpendicular to that on which it stands.

Definition 15: A circle is a plane figure contained by one line such that all the straight lines falling upon it from one point among those lying within the figure are equal to one another.

Definition 23: Parallel straight lines are straight lines which, being in the same plane and being produced indefinitely in both directions, do not meet one another in either direction.

Postulates

Postulate 1: It is possible to draw a straight line from any point to any point.

Postulate 2: It is possible to produce a finite straight line continuously in a straight line. (That is, if you have drawn a finite line segment, you can always extend it further.)

Postulate 3: It is possible to describe a circle with any center and radius. (This assumes the use of a compass.)

Postulate 4: All right angles are equal to one another.

Postulate 5: If a straight line falling on two straight lines makes the interior angles on the same side less than two right angles, the two straight lines, if produced indefinitely, meet on that side on which the angles are less than the two right angles.

The First Challenge

Homework Assignment

Proposition 1: Construct an equilateral triangle on a given finite straight line.

Your task is to prove that this construction is possible using only Euclid's definitions and postulates. You may use GeoGebra to help visualize the construction, but your solution should be a rigorous written argument that follows the logical structure established by Euclid.

Remember: You can only use the tools explicitly allowed by Euclid's postulates - essentially a straightedge (for drawing lines) and a compass (for drawing circles). No measuring devices or other modern tools are permitted in the formal proof.

Conclusion

From ancient Babylonian clay tablets to Euclid's systematic axiomatization, we see the evolution of mathematics from computational technique to rigorous logical science. The transition from accepting empirical observations to demanding formal proofs represents one of humanity's greatest intellectual achievements. Mathematics at its core is not merely about calculating or measuring - it is about discovering eternal truths through the power of logical reasoning, built upon carefully chosen foundations that we accept as self-evident.

This axiomatic approach ensures that our mathematical knowledge stands on solid ground, protected from the logical quicksand that could undermine less carefully constructed systems of thought. Every theorem we prove, every relationship we establish, traces its validity back through an unbroken chain of logical steps to these fundamental axioms that form the bedrock of mathematical truth.
